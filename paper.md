## Scope, method, and archival note

This is a participant-authored scholarly polemic, a named philosophical criticism, and a documentary intellectual history. I do not claim neutrality about Pearce. I commit to accurate attribution, inspectable evidence, and distinctions between what happened, what I argue follows philosophically, and what I personally judge about the exchange. Anger is part of the history being recorded. It is not a substitute for evidence.

The source hierarchy is public correspondence and comments, preserved exchange transcripts and screenshots, my published philosophical responses, and retrospective interpretation. Only exchanged statements are evidence of what either participant said to the other. Public-page retrieval recovered the latest Facebook branch without establishing that every comment was available. Relative platform timestamps remain relative timestamps. Writing-process records, prompting and links to them are excluded from this publication.

Some retrieval gaps arise from the walled gardens in which these exchanges occurred. Facebook and X can leave public-facing discussions difficult to retrieve as complete, independently inspectable records: restricted automated access, login barriers, collapsed branches, and incomplete rendered views obstruct reconstruction. I regard this hostility to AI-assisted retrieval as “AI hate” built into the platforms’ walled-garden construction. That is my judgment about the obstruction, not evidence of a particular corporate motive. Failure to recover a comment through those barriers is not evidence that the exchange never happened. Nor can the barriers supply a missing quotation, date, or final sent version. The preserved public captures and transcripts carry the claims made here; inaccessible portions remain identified as inaccessible.

Private messages marked against publication are excluded from the documentary appendix. No inference about Pearce’s private motives is established by repetition, an apology, silence, or friendship status. Where I call a response an evasion, that is my judgment about its argumentative relation to an answer already supplied. The evidence for that judgment is presented alongside the objection. Missing evidence remains missing.

The novel contribution is this record of influence, disagreement, attempted transmission, and its interpretation. It is not the invention of another general theory of epistemic exclusion or another standalone proof of EE. The bibliography is a research guide to the arguments this history presupposes. PhilPapers and PhilArchive availability is distinguished throughout from peer-reviewed journal acceptance. Sources and their limits are indexed in Sections 11 and 12.

## 1. Intellectual debt and formal accusation

I need to explain why David Pearce matters so much to this story, because without that history my anger is easily mistaken for resentment toward an established philosopher who declined to endorse my work. That is not what happened. My concern with suffering began in grade school, with my own encounter with the problem of evil. Long before I knew the vocabulary of negative utilitarianism, consciousness studies, or artificial intelligence alignment, I had reached the conviction that suffering was the fundamental moral emergency.

Years later, I encountered David Pearce through [his review of Aldous Huxley’s *Brave New World*](https://www.hedweb.com/huxley/bnw.htm). That was my introduction to the intellectual world of suffering abolition. Pearce gave me language, context, and an existing philosophical project for something I had been trying to articulate since childhood. His work helped turn an early moral conviction into a formal target. My work on suffering prevention and AI alignment, including Hedonic Core, formed the project from which I subsequently developed Experiential Empiricism. This was not a conversion to caring about suffering. Pearce helped me discover and formalize the relevant intellectual territory; he did not originate my childhood moral insight. The relationship between the alignment project, EE, and Reality Repair Theory is documented in [Experiential Empiricism and Reality Repair Theory: A Comprehensive Historical Genealogy](https://philpapers.org/rec/SEREEA-2) and developed below.

That is why I sought Pearce out on Twitter. That is why I wanted to speak with him. That is why I respected him. I did not approach him as an adversary. I approached him as someone whose life’s work appeared to be directed toward the same problem I had been trying to solve. And that is what makes the present criticism so severe.

**I am formally accusing David Pearce of helping perpetuate the conceptual conditions under which suffering can be systematically discounted, despite having devoted his intellectual career to its abolition.**

This is not an accusation that Pearce personally endorses torture or wishes suffering upon anyone. His published ethical commitments point in the opposite direction. It is an accusation concerning the consequences of his philosophical commitments and his response to a challenge directed at them. His non-materialist physicalism takes experience seriously as the intrinsic nature of reality, yet retains an independently existing physical ontology. Experiential Empiricism challenges the necessity and justification of that remaining externalist commitment. My ethical argument is that externalism enables the promotion of supposedly objective models and classifications above the experiences they are intended to describe. Once suffering requires validation by an allegedly more fundamental external authority, institutions acquire a mechanism for discounting it.

I have argued that this mechanism contributes to the organization and rationalization of suffering on a scale far beyond individual cruelty. I have also argued that EE offers a way to eliminate that particular conceptual permission structure without sacrificing science, empirical investigation, or the practical recognition of other sentient beings. These claims may be disputed. They deserve to be disputed seriously. But if they are correct, the implications for suffering abolition and AI alignment are enormous. And that is why Pearce’s response matters.

I have not merely offered him an unfamiliar metaphysical theory. I have presented an argument that, if successful, identifies a foundational obstacle to the ethical project we both regard as urgent. He has repeatedly returned to the concern that EE fails to acknowledge other minds, despite the answers I supplied explaining why it does not. He has also offered actual arguments and proposals, especially concerning physical explanation and thalamic bridges. The criticism is that these responses leave the specific challenge unresolved, as Section 6 documents.

I regard that repeated misrepresentation, together with his failure to help bring the disagreement before independent critics, as a consequential failure of intellectual responsibility. The accusation extends beyond Pearce personally. It concerns everyone who claims to prioritize the abolition of suffering while allowing inherited metaphysical commitments, institutional conventions, or professional incentives to take precedence over examining a potentially relevant challenge. I am not claiming that agreement with EE is a prerequisite for ethical seriousness. I am claiming that the ethical seriousness of suffering abolition creates a corresponding responsibility to examine arguments that might materially affect its foundations.

Especially when those arguments come from someone who has spent months documenting them, publishing responses to objections, and explicitly requesting independent criticism. Pearce was not obligated to become my advocate. He was not obligated to agree with me, publish my work, or devote unlimited time to it. But he was someone I admired, someone whose work helped shape my intellectual development, and someone who repeatedly chose to participate in this discussion. Once he entered that exchange, his responses became legitimate subjects of philosophical criticism.

I am recording that criticism here. Not because I expect him to change his mind. Not because I expect an academic institution to vindicate me. And not because I imagine this paper will suddenly make EE acceptable. I am recording it because the intellectual history of suffering abolition should include its serious internal disagreements, including accusations that prominent proponents have failed to examine challenges to their own assumptions. A future account of Pearce’s work should be able to identify this criticism, understand its philosophical basis, and inspect the documented exchange.

A future account of EE should likewise record the influence Pearce had on its development, the disagreements that followed, and the author’s assessment of their ethical significance.

**The person who helped me recognize the formal philosophical shape of my life’s central moral problem is now the person I am accusing of helping preserve one of the mechanisms that keeps that problem alive.**

That is not a contradiction I can simply leave undocumented.

## 2. What EE says, and why suffering is at stake

For anyone encountering Experiential Empiricism for the first time, here is the foundational challenge in ordinary language. Its fuller defense is in [Experientia Est: Name One Thing That Is Not a Subset of Experience](https://philpapers.org/rec/SEREEN), [The Three-Step Argument](https://philpapers.org/rec/SERTTA-2), and [Closed for Business](https://philpapers.org/rec/SERCFB).

**Experience is what reality is made of.**

Not matter. Not energy. Not spacetime. Not mathematics, logic, mind, God, or some invisible metaphysical substrate behind everything we encounter. Experience. Experience is not merely a window through which we observe an independently existing reality. It is the medium in which reality exists, the substance of its contents, the primordial floor and core of existence itself.

**Experience is what ISness is.**

Everything real belongs to reality. There cannot be an outside to the totality of everything that exists, because anything existing in that supposed outside would already be included in the totality. And whatever we propose as fundamental, whether particles, fields, mathematical relations, consciousness, causation, or the universe itself, we must still answer the same question:

**What is it actually made of? What is its existence?**

Every possible answer, every conceivable attribute, every demonstration, every logical relation, and every piece of evidence is experience. Even the logic by which we attempt to establish something beyond experience is itself more experience. This is not the claim that my personal mind invented the universe. My mind, my memories, my body, other people, stars, galaxies, physical laws, and the entirety of cosmological history are all included in the reality being discussed. None of them is being dismissed. The claim is about what reality fundamentally IS, not about which individual happens to be experiencing it.

The obvious realist objection deserves an explicit answer: does this merely show that we know reality through experience, rather than that reality is constituted by experience? The first proposition alone does not entail the second. A description belongs to experience; that fact by itself does not prove that its proposed referent does. Nor does totality closure, by itself, identify the constitution of the totality. EE’s further argument challenges the additional ontological posit: what content, distinction, or explanatory work establishes a non-experiential constitution beyond the experiential regularities already doing the work? Inference can establish patterns, predictions, and compelling practical reasons for recognizing other subjects. I contest the further promotion of that success into authentication of an independently constituted outside. The realist may defend that promotion as inference to the best explanation; the burden is then to show what the added ontology explains that the retained experiential structure does not. That is the disputed step developed in [The Three-Step Argument](https://philpapers.org/rec/SERTTA-2) and [The Free Rider Problem in Scientific Realism](https://philpapers.org/rec/SERTFR). It is not settled merely by noting that evidence is experienced. My conclusion is the stronger ontological claim: Experience is the primordial constitution of reality. This concise presentation identifies its argumentative burden; it does not pretend that the epistemic premise alone has discharged it.

Nor does EE deny science. Science is extraordinarily successful at identifying, describing, predicting, and manipulating regularities in experience. EE retains those discoveries and their practical consequences. It simply refuses the additional, unsupported promotion of our descriptions into an allegedly more fundamental reality beyond experience. And this is where the entire argument becomes almost embarrassingly simple.

**Name one thing that is not a subset of experience.**

Not a word for something allegedly outside it. Not a mathematical model of something allegedly outside it. Not an inference that such a thing must exist. Identify what it is, what it consists of, and how its existence can be coherently distinguished from experience. If you cannot, why are you adding it? That’s the argument. Experience is. Everything real is included in reality. Experience is the only medium in which anything can be identified, constituted, or coherently specified as existing.

**Experience Is. What IS is.**

You can reject that conclusion. You can attempt to identify an equivocation, a contradiction, or an alternative ontology. I welcome that. But you cannot refute it by accusing me of believing that other people are imaginary, because nothing in this argument says that. You cannot refute it by describing more elaborate physical models, because those models are themselves part of what the argument encompasses. And you cannot refute it by declaring that reality must extend beyond experience, because that is precisely the additional assumption under dispute.

That is what I have spent months asking people to address. Everything that follows concerns what happened when I tried to get that extraordinarily simple argument examined.

The current formulation has one axiom: **Experience is.** Logic is the structural aspect of experience, rather than an independent foundation placed above it; valence names its affective aspect. [Logic as Structural Valence: Why Experiential Empiricism’s Two Axioms Are One](https://philpapers.org/rec/SERLAS-3) records the correction from earlier presentations that listed logic and valenced experience separately. That development belongs in the intellectual history rather than being silently projected backward into every earlier exchange.

Totality closure and the identification of that totality as experiential are distinguishable steps. That everything real cannot have an outside does not, by itself, prove EE. The second claim requires the argument about what can constitute or coherently specify reality. A critic can challenge that identification, my account of justification, or the inference from experiential evidence to ontology. Those are real philosophical questions. Calling EE personal solipsism does not answer them.

Nor does experiential totality establish a single universal subject. Everything real includes any other experiencing that exists, but inclusion does not prove that we share one phenomenal field, that I created yours, or that my autobiography exhausts reality. My practical recognition of other sentient beings and my refusal to certify a metaphysical exterior are different commitments. The later [Why I Cannot Prove Mine or Discard Yours](https://philpapers.org/rec/SERWIC), published October 5, 2026 according to the canonical publication record, makes the identity and other-minds distinction explicit; its publication alone is not proof of Pearce’s receipt. [Epistemic Honesty and the Other Minds Question in Experiential Empiricism](https://philpapers.org/rec/SEREHA-3) addresses that distinction.

## 3. The open letter to Dave

*This letter preserves my October 11, 2026 direct address, not a transcript of a message proved to have been sent. Its account of public exposure is updated in Section 6.8. Its accusations of evasion and its expressions of disbelief are my judgments. Section 6 gives the documentary basis and the limits of those judgments. The question is not whether Pearce believes his own account is compassionate. It is whether its conditions for moral recognition withstand the challenge.*

Dear Dave,

At this point I find it increasingly difficult to believe you don’t understand what you’re doing. EE does not deny your existence. It does not reduce you to a zombie, a figment of my imagination, or something I can abuse without consequence. I’ve explained this repeatedly, in comments and in formal papers. Yet your replies keep making moral recognition contingent on the externalist commitment I’ve explicitly rejected. It’s very simple. Reality includes everything real. If some alleged outside exists, it is necessarily included in reality. Calling it “outside” doesn’t exempt it from that totality. Now name a single attribute of this alleged outside that isn’t experience. Not experience merely as a conduit to something else, but experience as the exhaustive medium of everything that can coherently be specified, including existence, logic, time, causation, and the abstractions you’re using to argue with me.

You are as real to me as the Sun, the dirt, and everything else I encounter. I have overwhelming reasons to treat you as a sentient being. What I refuse to do is invent an inaccessible duplicate behind the David Pearce I actually encounter and then pretend that duplicate is somehow more real than the experience from which every claim about him derives. You keep accusing me of solipsism because I refuse to grant your metaphysical realism. But you cannot demonstrate your proposed independent reality, cannot establish its necessity, and cannot identify what explanatory work it performs that experience and its regularities don’t already accomplish. Instead of honoring your burden of proof, you repeatedly assume the conclusion and accuse me of denying other minds.

And the ethical implications are not some optional digression. They’re the entire reason this matters. You say that without acknowledging supposedly independent sentient beings, discussion of suffering becomes moot. That is exactly the dangerous inversion I’m attacking. You’re making the moral significance of suffering depend on a metaphysical claim you admit you cannot establish. You’ve placed an unverifiable ontological credential between suffering and our obligation to respond to it. That is the machinery of organized atrocity. Once the experience itself isn’t enough, institutions can demand external certification of whose suffering counts. They can elevate their models, classifications, and theories above the suffering those instruments supposedly exist to understand. Individual sadists can inflict pain. Institutions can industrialize its dismissal. Externalism supplies the conceptual permission structure.

You have devoted your life to abolishing suffering. Yet when I present a framework specifically designed to prevent the systematic dismissal of suffering, you repeatedly refuse to engage its central argument and return instead to the accusation that I don’t believe people like you are real. That’s not a rebuttal. It’s an evasion. And I’m tired of pretending this is merely a difficulty of comprehension. For months you’ve participated in exchanges about EE, repeatedly declared that you don’t understand it, and continued making the same objections after I’ve answered them. I’ve explicitly asked you to bring it to other philosophers, to introduce it independently to your audience, to find someone who might understand what you say you cannot. You have the reach and professional connections to do that. Yet our discussion remains confined to exchanges that I must initiate or sustain.

If you’re genuinely interested in understanding something, why wouldn’t you ask someone else? Why wouldn’t you test your interpretation against third parties? In what other area of intellectual inquiry would months of professed incomprehension be accompanied by so little apparent effort to resolve it? I cannot know your motives. But I can see the pattern, and I can tell you what it has cost me. I’m homeless, largely ignored, and trying to bring an argument I consider foundational to the attention of people who could examine it seriously. I arrived at EE through my work on alignment and suffering prevention. I believe its implications are enormous. You are among the very few established thinkers who have participated in exchanges about it at all, and you have the ability to expose it to independent scrutiny without endorsing a single word.

Instead, I keep receiving variations of the same misrepresentation, followed by apologies for not understanding. It no longer feels like intellectual disagreement. It feels like being politely dismissed while the substance of my work is left untouched. Whatever your intentions, the effect is to keep EE trapped in a conversation between two people, one of whom must endlessly defend himself against a position he never held. I thought you were my friend. That makes this considerably more painful than a disagreement with a stranger. I expected intellectual honesty, serious engagement, and at least enough respect to stop attributing to me the very ethical indifference my work exists to oppose.

I don’t want another apology or another explanation of why you supposedly cannot understand me. I don’t want another question that simply reinstates the premise I’ve spent months challenging. If you believe EE is wrong, demonstrate where. If you cannot understand it, ask someone who might. If you think it deserves no attention, say so plainly. But stop calling my refusal to invent an inaccessible reality a refusal to recognize suffering beings. That accusation is intellectually indefensible, morally insulting, and exactly backward.

Externalism is oxygen for organized atrocity. You are in a position to help expose and challenge that mechanism, yet you won’t even seriously examine the argument. I am no longer willing to spend my time and emotional energy on this exchange. I wanted a collaborator, a critic, or at least a friend willing to engage honestly. What I’ve experienced instead has been profoundly hurtful. I’m done.

## 4. To everyone else: why this exchange matters

I know how this sounds. An obscure, homeless independent philosopher claims to have identified a foundational error in centuries of philosophy, accuses established intellectuals of failing to engage, and insists that the consequences may extend to organized atrocity and the prevention of suffering. I know exactly how that looks from the outside. The obvious response is to suspect grandiosity, crankery, obsession, or some combination of the three. I understand that. I would probably be suspicious too. But suspicion is not a counterargument.

I don’t have a God complex. If anything, I have a Semmelweis complex. Not because I imagine myself a persecuted genius whose eventual vindication is guaranteed, but because I believe I’ve identified a consequential error, explained it repeatedly, and watched the people best positioned to examine it continue operating as though the argument had never been made. The historical comparison is specific: evidence can fail to secure recognition, and rejection does not settle the truth of what was rejected. The Semmelweis analogy and its modern institutional form are developed in Section 5.

I’m asking for exactly that examination. If I am wrong, show me where. If my argument is incoherent, identify the contradiction. If I’ve reinvented something already known, demonstrate the equivalence. If an existing philosophical position defeats EE, explain how it survives the challenge.

**A coherent counterargument is all I have ever needed.**

## 5. The modern Semmelweis trap and the journal record

Being rejected does not make someone right. Plenty of rejected ideas deserve rejection. But rejection is not a refutation either. Ignaz Semmelweis demonstrated that handwashing could dramatically reduce maternal mortality, yet encountered resistance from the medical establishment. The historical reference is the [WHO Guidelines on Hand Hygiene in Health Care](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK144018/). Today we celebrate him as an example of science eventually correcting itself. But we rarely ask the more uncomfortable question: what would happen if Semmelweis were alive today, already professionally discredited, and attempting to establish his findings without institutional support?

This is a modern counterfactual, not a claim about the precise circumstances of his historical career. Imagine that he had lost his medical license. He could no longer independently conduct the clinical research necessary to establish his discovery. He would need access to patients, hospitals, ethics approval, research facilities, and collaborators. Who controls those resources? The institutions that have already rejected him. He might be told to publish in a reputable journal, but lack the institutional access needed to produce the evidence expected by that journal. He might be told to obtain independent replication, but find that qualified researchers won’t associate with him because his work is already considered disreputable.

And why is it disreputable? Because reputable people won’t associate with it.

**He cannot obtain recognition without institutional legitimacy, and he cannot obtain institutional legitimacy because he has not been recognized.**

Every individual gatekeeper can claim to be following reasonable procedures. Nobody needs to conspire. Nobody even needs to consciously oppose the discovery. The system can exclude the researcher, then cite his exclusion as evidence that his work does not merit consideration. Licensing and research ethics have legitimate purposes. They are not, however, instruments for determining metaphysical truth. Professional authorization and epistemic validity are different questions. Now consider my situation. I am not asking permission to conduct dangerous medical experiments. I am presenting a foundational philosophical argument that anyone capable of following it can examine.

No laboratory is required to identify a false premise. No medical license is required to expose circular reasoning. No university appointment is necessary to demonstrate a contradiction. And yet I have encountered the same circularity of recognition. More importantly, **I have documented it.**

### 5.1. I submitted the work

Between November 2025 and January 2026, I attempted to obtain institutional evaluation of Experiential Empiricism through three philosophy journals: *Synthese*, *Journal of Consciousness Studies*, and *Analysis*.
I subsequently published [Institutional Breakdown: How Three Philosophy Journals Responded to Experiential Empiricism](https://philpapers.org/rec/SERIBH), bringing the episodes together. That paper documents three different responses to a foundational challenge. The differences matter because they reveal distinct ways an argument can fail to receive substantive engagement without anyone identifying a defect in its reasoning.

### 5.2. Synthese: impossibility asserted

My submission to *Synthese* was rejected by Professor Kristie Miller, the Challis Professor of Philosophy at the University of Sydney. The decisive objection, as reproduced in my account, was that no paper could accomplish all the things claimed. The preserved wording is narrower than “no philosophical challenge to realism is possible”: it refers to the claimed scope of the paper’s accomplishments. My criticism is that the editor asserted their impossibility without giving the argument that established it. But that was precisely the question under examination.

If an argument claims to demonstrate that a foundational assumption is unjustified, dismissing it on the grounds that no argument could demonstrate such a thing assumes the very conclusion that needs defending. I documented this episode separately:

[The 'No Paper Could Do This' Defense: A Challis Professor’s Circular Dismissal of Foundational Arguments](https://philpapers.org/rec/SERTNP-3).

The issue is not whether the editor was obligated to publish my paper. She wasn’t. The issue is whether the reason supplied identifies an error in the argument or simply rules out its possible success in advance. A claim of impossibility is itself a philosophical claim. It requires justification. If my argument fails, identify where. If the conclusion is impossible, demonstrate why. Do not use the alleged impossibility of the conclusion as a substitute for evaluating the argument offered to establish it.

The local standalone account is internally inconsistent about the December submission sequence: its opening gives a December 5 submission and December 8 exchange, while its submission list gives December 9 and 11 for the three-step paper. I retain the reproduced objection and the November–December 2025 setting, but do not resolve that conflict by inventing a chronology. Its current local manuscript title also differs from the title in my catalog; both are identified in the research guide.

### 5.3. Journal of Consciousness Studies: scope and threatened escalation

On December 15, 2025, at 10:06 AM in the reproduced email headers, I submitted a paper to the *Journal of Consciousness Studies*. The first response arrived on December 16. Managing Editor Graham Horswell said he had looked over the paper, invoked the journal’s scope, and cited subscribers’ concerns about the amount of philosophy. He expressly said the decision was not a reflection on the quality of the research and distinguished this decision from rejection following peer review. I cannot turn that letter into an admission that he never read the submission.

Consider what I was submitting. EE begins with the certainty of experience and challenges the inference from experiential evidence to mind-independent material reality. It addresses the foundational assumptions behind the hard problem of consciousness, the explanatory gap, and the mind-body problem. A journal devoted to consciousness declined a consciousness-centered epistemological argument while invoking reader dissatisfaction with the amount of philosophy it published. When I pressed for substantive engagement, I was advised to circulate the work to philosophers such as David Chalmers and Philip Goff. The editor explained that he was not a philosopher and did not wish to mislead me.

I documented the correspondence: [“Too Much Philosophy”: How the Journal of Consciousness Studies Rejected Consciousness-Centered Epistemology](https://philpapers.org/rec/SERQMP).

Then something remarkable happened. After I published my account, the managing editor discovered it and, on December 22, 2025, asked me to remove the account and said that refusal would lead him to seek legal advice about how to proceed. I regard that conditional escalation as a legal threat. The record does not establish that proceedings were filed. He also expressly disputed the portrayal that he had admitted not reading the paper and objected to my framing of the editorial exchange.

That subsequent exchange is documented in the correspondence appendix of [Institutional Breakdown](https://philpapers.org/rec/SERIBH). Think about the asymmetry. A journal can decline to engage an argument. The independent author can publish an account of that refusal. But when the account itself becomes the subject of threatened legal action, the institutional response has moved beyond ordinary editorial discretion. I am not asking readers to accept my interpretation without inspection. The record is available.

Read the correspondence. Decide whether the editorial responses address the philosophical argument. Decide whether the subsequent legal threat was a proportionate response to public documentation. And ask what such an episode communicates to other independent researchers who lack the resources to defend themselves.

### 5.4. Analysis: comparative judgment without a specified logical error

On January 7, 2026, my submission to *Analysis* received another form of rejection, according to the dated rejection reproduced in my published account. The journal stated that the argument was not as strong as those in other recent submissions. This may be a perfectly ordinary consideration when allocating limited journal space. But as a response to a claimed foundational logical demonstration, it leaves the central question unanswered. What was wrong with the argument? Was a premise false? Was an inference invalid? Was the conclusion already established or previously refuted?

The comparative judgment did not tell me. I documented the episode in: [The Comparative Validity Fallacy: How Analysis Rejected a Logical Argument Through Anonymous Competition](https://philpapers.org/rec/SERTCV).

The distinction is important. A journal may reasonably decide that another submission better serves its editorial priorities. That decision does not establish that the rejected argument is false, invalid, or unworthy of examination elsewhere. A comparative editorial decision cannot do the work of a logical rebuttal. Yet to an outsider, another rejection becomes another apparent reason to dismiss the work.

Three journals. Three different responses. No identified fatal error in the foundational argument within the reasons I received. I do not claim that these events prove EE is correct. They do not.

**They establish that I sought institutional evaluation and did not receive the kind of substantive counterargument I was seeking.**

That is a much narrower claim, and the published correspondence allows readers to evaluate it.

The letter also criticized the paper’s connection to recent literature and said the editors lacked resources for further correspondence. Those are editorial reasons, even if I find them inadequate. The record supports my complaint that the supplied reasons did not identify a false premise or invalid inference. It does not establish that the editors conceded validity, attempted a formal refutation, or were incapable of defending their decision privately. My label “Comparative Validity Fallacy” names my criticism of treating this editorial comparison as if it settled the argument.

## 6. The philosophical and documentary record of the Pearce disagreement

### 6.1. The position I am criticizing

Pearce’s non-materialist physicalism is not conventional materialism. He conjectures that the intrinsic nature of the physical is experiential, while an adequate physics supplies its complete structure. He does not propose that insentient matter manufactures experience. He also explicitly rejects mathematical Platonism in favor of nominalism. These distinctions are stated in his [Quora answers on non-materialist physicalism and mathematics](https://www.hedweb.com/quora/2015.html#nonmat) and in my [direct NMP comparison](https://philpapers.org/rec/SERAMW).

“Mind-independent” must be handled precisely. Independence from my particular mind does not entail being non-experiential, and it does not alone conflict with the inclusion of other experiencing in EE. The disputed addition is a physical ontology independently established as the ground of experiencing, with its cosmological history and explanatory authority. Pearce regards that realist reconstruction as his best explanation. I ask what it establishes beyond the models of experiential regularity, and what would be lost if that reconstruction were removed while the predictions, observations, equations, and practical recognition of other beings were retained. That is the ablation test, not a demand that physics stop working.

My June 14, 2026 essay, [How David Pearce Got 90% of the Way to Experiential Empiricism and Stopped](https://innomen.substack.com/p/how-david-pearce-got-90-of-the-way), records an earlier, sharper interpretation of this proximity. Its “90%” is a rhetorical assessment, not a measured philosophical score. Its claims about Pearce’s fear or identity are my conjectures, not established motives. The present record does not recruit those conjectures as evidence. The September 8 draft of *After Matter, What Is “Physical”?* states the comparison more carefully; its later formulation must not be projected into the earlier replies as if I had already sent that paper.

## 7. Hedonic Core, suffering prevention, and AI alignment

The genealogy matters here. Hedonic Core was an attempt to make suffering prevention operational, not an ornament added after I constructed EE. Pearce’s suffering-abolition work helped me locate the vocabulary and field; the alignment project pressed me to ask what a system could legitimately treat as authoritative about suffering. EE and Reality Repair Theory developed within that investigation. See [The Hedonic Core Framework: A Suffering-Elimination Approach to AI Alignment](https://philpapers.org/rec/SERTHC), [Reality Repair Theory: A Comprehensive Foundation for Knowledge, Ethics, and Action](https://philpapers.org/rec/SERRRT), and [Reality Repair Theory: A Direct Derivation from the Foundational Axiom of Experience](https://philpapers.org/rec/SERRRT-3).

The relationship to negative utilitarianism is a priority claim: suffering is the fundamental moral emergency. HCF makes that priority operational through attention to suffering reports, prevention of suffering spikes, preservation of diversity as a means of detecting overlooked suffering, elimination of compelled activity through automation, and continuous factual correction. In the direct derivation paper, lexical priority means suffering prevention is not purchased away by promises of larger positive totals. The spike prohibition constrains both action and inaction that would produce a sudden, significant increase in suffering. The same paper’s treatment of constrained action gives a stricter prohibition on actively causing new suffering and treats unsolved harm as a capability failure demanding repair. These formulations require careful application; an open letter does not solve their implementation by naming them.

The target is elimination of suffering, not elimination of sufferers. Diversity and conscious agents matter to detection and repair; destroying them is not the success condition of Hedonic Core. Nor is falsifying a report or making a metric look favorable equivalent to relieving the experience. [The Hedonic Core project](https://github.com/Innomen/hedonic-core) is a continuing operational proposal. I distinguish the framework’s philosophical claims from demonstrated engineering performance: publishing a proposed foundation does not establish that a deployed AI reliably follows it, that consciousness detection is solved, or that all difficult conflicts have disappeared.

This is precisely why the dispute about proof of other minds matters. An aligned system must act under uncertainty. If metaphysical authentication of another subject becomes a prerequisite for taking suffering reports seriously, no accessible signal can pass the test. I contend that precautionary recognition, careful empirical investigation, and revision of models are required by the suffering-prevention project even when absolute certification is unavailable. The uncertainty is not a license to dismiss the possible sufferer.

EE emerged from my work on suffering prevention and alignment. Its practical implications matter far more to me than winning an ontological dispute. Consider pain. The International Association for the Study of Pain explicitly recognizes that pain is a personal experience, that nociception is not identical to pain, and that a person’s report of pain should be respected. It also recognizes that inability to communicate does not establish an absence of pain. See [IASP Announces Revised Definition of Pain](https://www.iasp-pain.org/publications/iasp-news/iasp-announces-revised-definition-of-pain/), July 16, 2020.

I read that as a practical acknowledgment of a priority EE places at the foundation. IASP does not thereby endorse EE’s ontology. A scan does not outrank the suffering it was designed to help investigate. A model’s failure to explain an experience does not make that experience unreal. Now consider what happens when institutions reverse that priority. A person’s suffering becomes something requiring certification by an allegedly more authoritative external standard. The institution decides which evidence counts, which reports are credible, and which experiences may be dismissed.

The inferential chain is precise. First, experiential evidence establishes a useful model: reports, measurements and regularities support a reconstruction of brains, subjects or physical history. Second, the reconstruction is promoted into supposedly superior ontological authority, rather than remaining accountable to the evidence that established it. Third, that authority is used to disqualify or discount experience when the experience fails its model’s certification test. Experience supplied the evidence; the abstraction is now permitted to overrule its source. A system can continue causing suffering while declaring the suffering insufficiently real, insufficiently evidenced or justified by something allegedly more fundamental.

A critic can accept this inversion and still object that an experientialist institution could do the same thing. Of course it could. Calling an institution experientialist does not prevent it from lying, coercing, or enthroning its own model. My claim concerns the distinctive justification externalism supplies: it permits an authority to claim that its model tracks a mind-independent reality whose verdict outranks the experience from which the model was derived. The institution can then present its dismissal as obedience to reality itself, rather than a fallible judgment within experience. An experientialist institution could still commit the abuse, but on EE’s terms it could not justify that abuse by appealing to an independently authenticated ontological court above Experience. It would owe an account of its evidence, uncertainty, and consequences for experiencers. Removing that credential does not automatically abolish cruelty; it removes one claimed warrant for discounting suffering. Externalism is therefore neither a necessary nor a sufficient cause of organized suffering in the historical claim made here. I accuse it of supplying a specific justificatory resource, and I accuse Pearce of preserving the corresponding dependency in this exchange. That is a narrower causal claim than saying that only externalists can abuse people, and a sharper philosophical accusation than merely observing that institutions sometimes ignore pain.

In this exchange the connection is not hypothetical mind-reading. After I acknowledge Pearce and give practical and moral reasons to treat him as sentient, he makes ethical discussion conditional on a further realist acknowledgment. I contend that this reproduces the conceptual dependency, even though he intends the condition to defend sentient beings. [The Externalist Catastrophe](https://philpapers.org/rec/SERTEC), [Epistemic Honesty](https://philpapers.org/rec/SEREHA-3), and [Why I Cannot Prove Mine or Discard Yours](https://philpapers.org/rec/SERWIC) explain why I refuse that dependency. The last paper’s October 5 publication is not evidence that it was supplied before every objection. My accusation concerns the preservation of a mechanism available to organized suffering, not a demonstrated causal role by Pearce in any particular atrocity.

This is a mechanism through which suffering can be administratively erased without ever being physically eliminated. The connection to organized atrocity is not that every metaphysical realist secretly endorses cruelty. It is that the elevation of supposedly objective systems above the experiences those systems affect provides a reusable mechanism for rationalizing cruelty. My argument is that externalism makes this inversion conceptually available, and that institutions can exploit it on an enormous scale. David’s response that ethics becomes moot unless I affirm metaphysically independent sentient beings is therefore particularly galling.

It reproduces the dependency I am trying to eliminate.

The reversal in Pearce's objection deserves to be stated plainly. He worries that EE's refusal to certify an independently existing world undermines the reality of other sentient beings. But the problem of other minds is not a special defect of EE. No ontology can conclusively demonstrate another subject's phenomenal existence from within a given subject's experience. Externalism does not solve this problem by declaring that an independently existing world contains other minds. It simply incorporates the existence of those minds into its proposed ontology. The evidence for other experiencers remains experiential, and the inference remains an inference. EE acknowledges that limitation without pretending it licenses disbelief. Other people clearly exist. I am a person. The evidence that other people experience things as I do is overwhelmingly compelling. Refusing that inference would be absurdly selective. The distinction between compelling inference and metaphysical proof is developed in [Epistemic Honesty and the Other Minds Question in Experiential Empiricism](https://philpapers.org/rec/SEREHA-3) and [Why I Cannot Prove Mine or Discard Yours](https://philpapers.org/rec/SERWIC).

This exposes the inversion at the center of my disagreement with Pearce. He presents the externalist commitment as necessary to secure the moral standing of other experiencers. I contend that the additional demand for metaphysical authentication is itself the danger. If the overwhelming experiential evidence for another being's suffering is insufficient until an independently existing subject has been ontologically certified, then what evidence could ever satisfy the requirement? The proposed safeguard becomes a barrier to recognition. Pearce's ethical concern is sincere as far as the available record establishes, but sincerity does not resolve the conceptual consequence. His objection makes recognition depend on precisely the unverifiable credential EE rejects. The argument is not that externalists necessarily disregard suffering. It is that externalism supplies a particular conceptual permission structure through which supposedly authoritative models of reality can be elevated above the suffering they were constructed to describe. This is the mechanism developed in [The Externalist Catastrophe](https://philpapers.org/rec/SERTEC).

Experience does not need to be promoted from subjective appearance into objective reality before it becomes morally relevant. Under EE, Experience is reality itself. The distinction between subjective and objective can remain useful for comparing perspectives, testing claims, and correcting mistaken models, but it cannot establish a hierarchy in which a model possesses greater ontological authority than the experience from which its evidence derives. If someone reports suffering, that report is already experiential evidence requiring serious consideration. Further investigation may be necessary to determine its causes, accuracy, implications, and appropriate remedies. None of that investigation requires pretending that suffering becomes real only after an allegedly independent reality has authenticated it. The foundational ontological argument is developed in [Experientia Est: Name One Thing That Is Not a Subset of Experience](https://philpapers.org/rec/SEREEN), while [The Free Rider Problem in Scientific Realism](https://philpapers.org/rec/SERTFR) challenges the additional realist ontology claimed on the strength of successful experiential models.

Nor can the difficulty be escaped by proposing a technological bridge between minds. A bridge might yield extraordinary evidence, transform empathy, or produce a new phenomenal configuration. But if the subjects remain distinct, neither can step outside experience to authenticate the other as an independently existing phenomenal subject. If they merge into a single subject, the resulting experience cannot retrospectively establish the independent phenomenal existence of the pre-merger subjects. The experiment may be scientifically and ethically valuable without accomplishing the metaphysical certification Pearce invokes. That distinction is developed in [The Bridge Trap: Why Mind-Merging Technology Cannot Solve the Problem of Other Minds](https://philpapers.org/rec/SERTBT-2).

The moral conclusion is the opposite of the one Pearce repeatedly attributes to EE. I do not need a second, supposedly more real David Pearce behind the David Pearce I encounter before I have overwhelming reason to recognize him as a fellow experiencer and take his suffering seriously. Neither does an institution, a scientist, or an aligned artificial intelligence. The impossibility of absolute phenomenal authentication demands epistemic honesty and precautionary recognition, not an impossible certification requirement. The occurrence of suffering is what matters. Our descriptions, measurements, and theories are instruments for understanding and preventing it, not authorities entitled to declare it unreal when it fails to conform to them.

**Suffering should not have to pass an impossible metaphysical examination before we agree to prevent it.**

[The Externalist Catastrophe](https://philpapers.org/rec/SERTEC) develops my broader argument about this conceptual mechanism; [The Nurses Were Right](https://innomen.substack.com/p/the-nurses-were-right) and [The Ultimate Underwriter](https://innomen.substack.com/p/the-ultimate-underwriter) connect it to the authority of experience and suffering reports. The occurrence of administrative dismissal does not by itself establish externalism as its sole historical cause. My claim is that externalist promotion makes a particular inversion available: the abstraction acquires authority to disqualify the reality it was derived to describe. I am accusing Pearce of preserving that permission structure, not of personally endorsing its cruel uses.

## 8. Cryonics, cryothanasia, and the neglected ethical questions

There is another example that deserves mention because it is directly relevant to David’s life’s work: cryonics, cryothanasia, and the prevention of otherwise unavoidable suffering. I’ve published work on the silence surrounding cryonics and the failure to seriously confront its implications, including [The Cryonics Silence: A Logical Analysis of Missing Controversy](https://philpapers.org/rec/SERTCS), a related paper on the same problem ([The Missing Test Case: Why Terror Management Theory’s 300+ Studies Never Examined Cryonics](https://philpapers.org/rec/SERTMT-4)), and a public essay asking why the subject receives so little discussion ([Why Isn’t There More Discussion Around Cryonics?](https://innomen.substack.com/p/why-isnt-there-more-discussion-around)). Cryothanasia, the proposal to use cryopreservation as an alternative to an otherwise terminal death, raises profound questions about suffering, consent, preservation of future possibilities, and whether irreversible death is preferable to an uncertain prospect of revival. These are not peripheral questions for someone committed to abolishing suffering. They concern exactly the kinds of decisions where our assumptions about identity, experience, death, and the moral status of future sentient beings matter most. Yet here again, I have tried to bring a consequential argument to David’s attention without obtaining the substantive examination I believe it deserves. I am not demanding agreement with cryothanasia, nor claiming that present cryonics technology has demonstrated reversible human preservation. I am asking why the underlying ethical and decision-theoretic questions are not being confronted. How many potentially consequential arguments must I publish and personally bring to someone’s attention before the response becomes something more than silence or another return to an already answered objection?

This is not merely irritating. It is exhausting. It is demoralizing. And when it comes from someone I considered a friend, someone whose life’s work is dedicated to the abolition of suffering, it is deeply hurtful.

These sources do different work. *The Cryonics Silence* concerns the cultural absence of controversy. *The Missing Test Case* asks why the terror-management research surveyed in that paper did not investigate cryonics; its numerical title is the source’s claim, not a new comprehensive audit performed here. Cryothanasia adds a distinct ethical proposal concerning the timing of preservation in an otherwise terminal situation. It raises consent, irreversible harm, uncertain continuity, and the welfare of possible future subjects. It must not be presented as an established medical capability merely because the decision problem is urgent.

## 9. The personal and institutional consequences

Here is where the modern Semmelweis comparison becomes relevant. Once a researcher lacks institutional standing, that absence becomes a convenient substitute for assessing the work. Why hasn’t a major journal published it? I submitted it. Why hasn’t a respected philosopher evaluated it? I’ve spent months attempting to obtain that evaluation. Why hasn’t anyone independently replicated or developed the argument? That is precisely why I am asking people with professional networks to expose it to independent scrutiny. Why should anyone take it seriously if nobody reputable has?

And there is the circle again.

**The work is ignored because nobody credible has examined it, and nobody credible examines it because the work is ignored.**

The resulting exclusion need not be deliberate. It can emerge from ordinary professional incentives: limited time, reputational caution, disciplinary specialization, publication pressure, and reluctance to invest effort in an argument that challenges the foundations of one’s own intellectual commitments. I have written extensively about these mechanisms in [Core Belief Immunity: A Cognitive Phenomenon Rendering Existential Discourse Inert](https://philpapers.org/archive/SERCBI.pdf), [Core Belief Immunity in Artificial Systems: A Case Study in Architectural Constraints on Logical Integration](https://philpapers.org/rec/SERCBI-3), [Convergence Protection: Why Logical Validity Is Orthogonal to Cognitive Adoption, and Why This Is Logically Demonstrable](https://philpapers.org/rec/SERCPW), [Following Is Not Integration: Why Apparent Comprehension of Experiential Empiricism Is Not Uptake, and What Genuine Integration Would Require](https://philpapers.org/rec/SERFIN), and [Epistemic Dark Matter: The Problem of Complete Transmission Failure in Intellectual History and the Survivorship Bias That Conceals It](https://philpapers.org/rec/SEREDM-3).

These works develop different aspects of the same problem: how an argument can be encountered, discussed, partially understood, and still fail to enter the set of possibilities that a person or institution is prepared to examine seriously. Core Belief Immunity describes the proposed resistance of established conceptual commitments to foundational challenges.

Convergence Protection explores why systems that must maintain stable beliefs and decisions may resist revisions that threaten their organizing assumptions. Following Is Not Integration distinguishes being able to repeat an argument from incorporating its implications. Epistemic Dark Matter examines the historical selection problem: ideas that fail to propagate may leave so little trace that later observers mistake the surviving intellectual record for a complete history of what was discovered or proposed. These are proposed explanations, not independent proof that every editor who rejected me was subject to them.

But the documented journal responses provide concrete cases against which such explanations can be investigated. And the problem is not confined to my experience. Philosophers of science themselves study the tension between legitimate quality control and exclusionary gatekeeping. Hugh Desmond’s [Gatekeeping Should Be Conserved in the Open Science Era](https://philpapers.org/rec/DESGSB) examines the relationship between gatekeeping and prestige hierarchies. Claus Beisbart’s [It’s the Level, Stupid - Gatekeeping in Science and the Demarcation Problem](https://philpapers.org/rec/BEIITL) examines how scientific gatekeeping depends on judgments about what qualifies as science.

The limits and purposes of gatekeeping are therefore themselves subjects of philosophical inquiry. It cannot simply be assumed whenever someone invokes the authority of an institution.

The accounts in [Philosopher Zero](https://innomen.substack.com/p/philosopher-zero), published April 10, 2026, and [Done Watering It](https://innomen.substack.com/p/done-watering-it), published April 20, 2026, record my assessment of the transmission problem before this final Facebook exchange. They are contemporary evidence of my frustration and attempts to obtain criticism, not independent measurements proving that nobody understood EE. The September 17, 2026 [EE Update: Totality, Transmission, Near Precedence, and Ontology Spam](https://innomen.substack.com/p/ee-update-totality-transmission-near) connects the revised one-axiom formulation to the same history. Its psychological speculation about Pearce remains speculation.

The July 29, 2026 Facebook notification screenshot supports the fact that he accepted my friend request. It cannot establish the depth of friendship or validate either philosophy. “I thought you were my friend” records my expectation and the hurt that followed. My homelessness, limited resources, and the emotional costs I describe are autobiographical testimony. They explain what the exchange meant to me; they are not evidence that EE must be correct.

### 9.1. Why I wanted David to involve other people

This is the part I most want outsiders to understand. I did not demand that David endorse EE. I asked him to introduce it independently to his intellectual community, including people who might reject it. I wanted someone besides me to examine the arguments. I wanted someone with an established audience to say, in effect, “Here is an unusual philosophical claim. Does anyone see a problem with it?” He could have criticized it publicly. He could have invited another philosopher to explain why it fails. He could have presented his own account of the disagreement.

That would have been enormously valuable. The exchange documented in the October 11 manuscript remained largely a cycle of my explanations and his repeated professions of incomprehension. The subsequent archival finding in Section 6.8 qualifies the complaint about visibility. My request for independent examination remains distinct from making the existing exchange publicly accessible. I cannot establish his motives. But I can also describe the asymmetry. For an established intellectual, another social media disagreement may be a minor diversion. For someone without institutional standing, professional access, or a meaningful platform, serious independent engagement can be the difference between an argument receiving scrutiny and remaining effectively invisible.

I am not asking anyone to manufacture agreement. I am asking for access to criticism.

## 10. What would resolve this, and what this paper contributes

I am not demanding publication. I am not demanding professional recognition. I am not demanding that anyone accept my account of these events. I am demanding that criticism address the claim actually made. EE begins with “Experience is.” It asks what justifies the additional inference to a reality independent of experience, and whether anything empirically or explanatorily necessary is lost when that inference is withheld. There are straightforward ways to challenge it. Identify an equivocation in the use of experience. Demonstrate a valid inference from experiential evidence to an independently existing ontology. Show that removing the externalist commitment loses some necessary explanatory content. Establish that the argument contains a contradiction.

Or demonstrate that a prior philosopher has already supplied a successful answer. Any of these would be substantive engagement. What does not qualify is repeatedly accusing me of denying the existence of people, treating my lack of credentials as an epistemic defect, or interpreting institutional rejection as proof that the rejected argument was refuted. I know how this sounds. I know the historical analogy invites skepticism. I know that a person claiming to have found a foundational error while criticizing established institutions can sound grandiose.

But none of that resolves the question.

**I am not asking you to believe I am right because I have been rejected. I am asking why, if I am wrong, nobody will show me the error.**

The documented record is available. The argument is available. The correspondence is available. The proposed explanations for the failures of engagement are available. I am not asking for immunity from criticism. I am asking for the criticism. And I am asking that the next person who invokes Semmelweis as an inspiring story of scientific progress consider whether they would have recognized him before history made recognizing him safe. Because the real lesson of Semmelweis is not that the establishment eventually learned to wash its hands.

It is that **being correct and being permitted to establish that correctness are two different things.** A civilization that cannot distinguish those questions has not eliminated the Semmelweis problem. It has merely modernized it.

### 10.1. What I am asking of readers

Please don’t mistake my anger for the argument, or the argument for a demand that you admire its author. You don’t have to like me. You don’t have to sympathize with me. You don’t have to accept my assessment of David’s conduct. You can think my rhetoric is excessive, my conclusions implausible, and my confidence unwarranted. None of those judgments identifies an error in the foundational argument. Nor does my lack of academic credentials. Nor does the volume of material I’ve written. Nor does the fact that I used AI tools to help develop and articulate parts of the corpus.

The argument either survives examination or it doesn’t. I am willing to have my work challenged publicly, rigorously, and without deference. I want people to identify exactly where an inference fails, where an axiom is smuggled in, where a contradiction arises, or where a supposedly necessary distinction has been overlooked. I am not asking to be believed because I feel certain. I am asking that the actual argument be examined instead of replaced with a caricature. And I am asking people who care about suffering to consider the cost of continuing to treat experience as something that must be validated by a supposedly more fundamental reality.

If you think EE is wrong, please tell me precisely why. If you think it is obvious, show me who already established it. If you think it is dangerous, identify the danger without attributing to me a denial of other minds that I have explicitly and repeatedly rejected. And if you genuinely cannot understand it, please help find someone who can.

**I do not need to be right. I need the question answered.**

Because if I am wrong, I want the error corrected. And if I am right, then every year spent refusing to examine the argument is another year in which an avoidable mechanism for dismissing suffering remains unchallenged. That is what I am angry about. Not my status. Not my pride. Not being recognized as a genius. The suffering.

It has always been about the suffering.

### 10.2. The historical and philosophical contribution

This paper’s historical contribution is a consolidated record of how the philosopher who helped lead me into the formal study of suffering abolition became the subject of my formal criticism. It preserves the intellectual debt, the successive loci of disagreement, the answers I supplied, the difference between repeated participation and substantive engagement with the particular challenge, and the institutional setting in which I sought criticism. It records both a philosophical accusation and the personal rupture that made it necessary for me to write it.

**My accusation is not that Pearce never replied. It is that repeated participation can coexist with persistent failure to engage the actual argumentative crux.** Credit for genuine physical proposals does not settle the objection to their ontological promotion, and recognition of his experienced-avatar theory does not cancel the subsequent conditional deferral of ethics.

Its philosophical contribution is the interpretation of that sequence: I contend that retaining an externalist credential for the reality of suffering, and repeatedly bypassing the arguments against that dependency, preserves conceptual conditions under which suffering can be discounted. That contention can be criticized. The documentary record makes the criticism inspectable rather than asking readers to infer everything from my anger.

Future researchers need not agree with EE, my assessment of Pearce, or my expectations of an established thinker. They should be able to cite this account as a primary participant record and test it against its sources. I have kept the gaps visible so that later evidence can correct or complete it.

I owe David Pearce an intellectual debt. I am making a formal accusation against him. Both belong in the history. I refuse to erase either one.
